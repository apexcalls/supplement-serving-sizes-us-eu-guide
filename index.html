Why Supplement Serving Sizes Differ Between the US and Europe

The same supplement can list a different serving size in the United States and in Europe, even when the product inside the bottle is identical. A buyer who compares the two labels may conclude that one version is stronger, or that one brand is more generous, when neither is true. The difference is in the rules, not in the product.

The first reason is that the two regions regulate supplements differently. In the United States, supplements are regulated as a category of food, and the label must follow the rules for a Supplement Facts panel. In the European Union, supplements are regulated as food supplements under a separate framework, with its own rules about what must be declared and how. Each framework defines serving sizes, nutrient reference values and permitted claims in its own way, so the same product ends up described differently on each side of the Atlantic.

The second reason is the reference values themselves. The United States uses Daily Values, while Europe uses Nutrient Reference Values, and the two do not always agree. A vitamin that has a Daily Value of 90 mg in the United States may have a Nutrient Reference Value of 80 mg in Europe. A product that provides 100 percent of the daily value in one market may provide 125 percent in the other, even though the amount in the capsule has not changed. The percentage on the label is a comparison against a local standard, not a measurement of the product itself.

The third reason is the maximum permitted levels. Europe sets maximum levels for vitamins and minerals in supplements, and those limits vary by country within the Union. The United States does not set equivalent maximums in the same way, so a product that is legal at one dose in America may need to be sold at a lower dose in Europe. The serving size is sometimes adjusted to fit the local limit, which means the European version of a product can contain less of an ingredient per serving than the American version, even when the brand and the formula are the same.

The fourth reason is labelling language. European rules require certain warnings and statements that American rules do not, and vice versa. A European label may carry a statement that the product is not a substitute for a varied diet, while an American label carries the familiar disclaimer that the statements have not been evaluated by the FDA. These differences change the look and the length of the label, and they can make the same product appear more or less cautious depending on which version you are reading.

The practical lesson for a buyer is to compare amounts, not percentages, and to compare serving sizes before comparing prices. Two bottles with the same price and the same number of capsules may offer very different value if one defines a serving as one capsule and the other as two. Serving sizes are a convention, not a property of the product. They are chosen by regulators and by brands, and they change across borders for reasons that have nothing to do with what is in the bottle. The buyer who reads the amount per serving, and divides the total contents by that amount, will always have a clearer picture than the buyer who trusts the percentage on the front. The label is not lying. It is speaking a local dialect, and it helps to know the translation.
